# Building a Portfolio and Managing Risk

## Asset allocation comes first

How you split money between equity, debt, gold and cash matters more than which individual stocks you pick. A common starting point is to keep an emergency fund of 6 months of expenses outside the market, then decide equity exposure by time horizon and risk tolerance:
- Money needed within 3 years usually should not be in stocks.
- For goals 7+ years away, a higher equity share is reasonable because short-term volatility has time to average out.

## Diversification

- Holding **15 to 25 stocks across 6+ sectors** removes most company-specific risk. Beyond that, extra stocks add little diversification.
- Avoid putting more than **10% to 15% in any single stock** or more than **25% to 30% in a single sector**.
- Stocks in the same sector (for example several private banks) tend to fall together, so owning many of them is not real diversification.
- An index fund on NIFTY 50 gives instant diversification across 50 large companies at very low cost.

## Measuring concentration

The **Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI)** of portfolio weights is the sum of squared weights. With weights as fractions, an equal-weight portfolio of N stocks has HHI = 1/N. The **effective number of holdings** = 1/HHI. A portfolio of 10 stocks where one stock is 60% behaves more like 2 to 3 stocks than 10.

## Position sizing

A disciplined rule: decide the maximum you are willing to lose on one idea (for example 1% to 2% of the portfolio), and size the position so that hitting your stop-loss costs no more than that.

Example: portfolio ₹1,00,000, risk per trade 1% = ₹1,000. Buying at ₹500 with a stop at ₹450 risks ₹50 per share, so buy 20 shares (₹10,000 position).

## Stop-losses and exits

- A stop-loss limits damage when you are wrong. Place it where the original reason for the trade would be invalid, not at an arbitrary round number.
- Write down why you bought a stock. Sell when that reason no longer holds, not only because the price moved.
- Averaging down on a falling stock without re-checking the thesis turns small losses into large ones.

## Drawdowns

A loss needs a bigger gain to recover: −10% needs +11%, −25% needs +33%, −50% needs +100%. Avoiding large drawdowns matters more than catching every rally.

## Rebalancing

Once or twice a year, bring allocations back to target by trimming winners and adding to underweight areas. This enforces "sell high, buy low" mechanically.

## Cash is a position

Holding cash is not a failure. It reduces risk and gives you capacity to buy during corrections. A portfolio that is 90% cash is very low risk but also earns little; a fully invested beginner portfolio carries full market risk.

## Behavioural traps

- **Loss aversion**: holding losers too long and selling winners too early.
- **FOMO**: buying after a stock has already run up because others are making money.
- **Overconfidence**: trading too often after a few wins; costs and taxes eat returns.
- **Anchoring**: fixating on the price you paid instead of the stock's current prospects.
- **Recency bias**: assuming the last few months will continue.

A written checklist before every trade (thesis, valuation, risk, position size, exit plan) is the simplest defence.

## Derivatives warning

SEBI's 2024 study found that about 93% of individual traders in equity futures and options lost money between FY22 and FY24. Leverage magnifies losses, and options lose value with time. Beginners should learn with delivery-based investing first.
