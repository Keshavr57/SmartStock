# Valuation and Financial Ratios

Ratios only mean something compared with the company's own history and with peers in the same sector. A P/E of 15 is cheap for an IT services company but normal for a PSU bank.

## Price to earnings (P/E)

P/E = share price ÷ earnings per share (EPS). It shows how many rupees investors pay for one rupee of annual profit.

- **Trailing (TTM) P/E** uses the last 12 months of earnings; **forward P/E** uses analysts' estimates.
- A high P/E means the market expects growth (or the stock is expensive); a low P/E can mean value, slow growth or a problem.
- P/E is not meaningful for loss-making companies and is distorted by one-off gains.
- Typical ranges in India vary widely: FMCG leaders often trade at 40 to 60, IT services at 20 to 30, private banks at 15 to 25, PSU banks and metals often below 12.

## PEG ratio

PEG = P/E ÷ expected annual earnings growth (%). A PEG near 1 suggests the valuation is in line with growth; well above 2 suggests growth is already priced in.

## Price to book (P/B)

P/B = price ÷ book value per share (net worth per share). Most useful for banks, NBFCs and asset-heavy businesses. For banks, compare P/B with return on equity: a bank earning 18% ROE deserves a much higher P/B than one earning 8%.

## EPS

Earnings per share = net profit ÷ number of shares. Growth in EPS over years matters more than the absolute number. Comparing EPS across companies is meaningless because share counts differ.

## ROE and ROCE

- **ROE** (return on equity) = net profit ÷ shareholders' equity. Above 15% sustained over years is considered good in India.
- **ROCE** (return on capital employed) = operating profit (EBIT) ÷ (equity + debt). Better than ROE for comparing companies with different debt levels; consistently above the cost of capital (around 12%) indicates value creation.
- High ROE driven by high debt is lower quality than high ROE with little debt.

## Margins

- **Gross margin**: revenue minus direct costs, as a share of revenue.
- **Operating (EBITDA or EBIT) margin**: profit from operations as a share of revenue.
- **Net margin**: final profit as a share of revenue.
Stable or expanding margins indicate pricing power; falling margins can signal competition or rising costs.

## Debt and liquidity

- **Debt to equity** = total debt ÷ shareholders' equity. Below 0.5 is conservative for non-financial companies; banks and NBFCs naturally run much higher leverage, so the ratio is not comparable for them.
- **Current ratio** = current assets ÷ current liabilities. Above 1 means short-term obligations are covered.
- **Interest coverage** = EBIT ÷ interest expense. Below 2 is a warning sign.

## Enterprise value multiples

- **EV/EBITDA** = (market cap + debt − cash) ÷ EBITDA. Useful to compare companies with different capital structures.
- **Price to sales** helps value companies that are not yet profitable, with caution.

## Dividend yield

Dividend yield = annual dividend per share ÷ share price. Utilities, PSUs, oil marketing and some IT companies often yield 2% to 5%. A very high yield can mean the price has collapsed, so check whether the dividend is sustainable (payout ratio and cash flow).

## Beta

Beta measures how much a stock moves relative to the market: 1.0 moves with the market, 1.5 moves 50% more, 0.5 moves half as much. Low-beta stocks (FMCG, pharma) are defensive; high-beta stocks (metals, realty, small caps) amplify market moves.

## Putting it together

A quick quality-and-value screen many Indian investors use: ROE and ROCE above 15% for several years, debt to equity below 0.5 (non-financials), consistent revenue and profit growth, positive operating cash flow close to net profit, and a P/E that is reasonable relative to growth and to sector peers.
